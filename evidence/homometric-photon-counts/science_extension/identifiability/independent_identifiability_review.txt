VERDICT

All six sections are mathematically sound under the written assumptions.
No correction is required to their deductions. The theorems establish
identification from complete population distributions and explicitly avoid
claiming stable reconstruction or a uniform finite-sample guarantee.

The assumptions that matter are known n=63, unit-modulus site coefficients,
the common clean null, independent Gaussian phase noise with finite positive
variance s, positive detector gain, and the previously established strict
fourth-moment gap for the two specific structures. The review below checks
the new arguments and independently checks the mean identity. It does not
repeat the entire exact geometry and cumulant calculation from the earlier
proof record.

1. PREMISES AND THEIR ROLE

The intensity bound follows from the triangle inequality. For independent
Gaussian phases, E exp(i sqrt(s) Z)=exp(-s/2). Hence

 E I_G(s) = n + exp(-s) sum_{j!=k} z_{G,j} conjugate(z_{G,k})
          = n + exp(-s)(|sum_j z_{G,j}|^2-n)
          = n(1-exp(-s))

at the common null. This verifies the mean used to recover s independently
of the detector argument. The prior factorization

 D4(s)=(455015761/20000) x^4(1-x)^5(x^3+3x^2+6x+6),
 x=exp(-s),

is strictly positive because 0<x<1. The present review uses the previously
proved identity, not a new numerical reconstruction of it. Equality of the
first three moments is not needed for Gaussian or Poisson joint
identifiability; the mean formula and strictly nonzero fourth gap suffice.
The first two common moments are needed for the equal-noise-variance
counterexample in section 6.

2. THEOREM 1: COMMON ADDITIVE ERROR

The local division of characteristic functions is valid: continuity and
phi_R(0)=1 guarantee a nonzero interval even when phi_R has zeros elsewhere.
Each compactly supported signal law has an entire characteristic function.
The union of two compact supports is bounded, so their difference also has
the stated entire extension. A real interval contains accumulation points
in the complex domain, and the complex identity theorem applies. Uniqueness
of characteristic functions completes the argument.

There is no hidden moment requirement on the error. No fourth moment of the
observed law is used. The conclusion applies to the same independent error
distribution under both structural comparisons. It is not joint
identification of an arbitrary unknown signal/error factorization. Section
6 correctly states this distinction.

3. LEMMA 2: EXACT INTENSITY SUPPORT

Every wrapped Gaussian with finite positive variance has a strictly
positive density on the circle, and independence yields full support on
the torus. Multiplication by deterministic unit phases is a translation
and does not change that support. The torus is connected and compact.
Its image under the continuous real-valued intensity map is therefore a
closed interval. Aligned phases give n^2 and the n-th roots of unity give
zero for n>=2. The whole image is exactly [0,n^2].

For any image point and any open interval containing it, the preimage is
nonempty and open and consequently has positive probability. This checks
the passage from a deterministic image to the probabilistic support,
including the endpoints. No positive point mass at either endpoint is
required. The singular limit s=0 and the uniform-phase limiting experiment
are correctly excluded from structural identification.

4. THEOREM 3: UNKNOWN GAUSSIAN PARAMETERS

For V=b+eta I, the support is exactly [b,b+eta n^2]. The logarithmic MGF
bounds hold for every real b and every positive t. Consequently log M_V(t)
is O(t), while the independent Gaussian contributes sigma^2 t^2/2.
The stated limit recovers sigma^2, including sigma=0. Restricting sigma to
be nonnegative then recovers sigma itself. All MGFs used are finite for
every real t in this model.

As an independent check, suppose two decompositions with compact signal
parts have different Gaussian variances. Dividing their characteristic
functions implies that one compact signal law is the convolution of the
other with a nondegenerate Gaussian. That convolution has a positive
density on the entire real line and cannot have compact support. Thus the
variances must agree. This confirms the MGF conclusion by a second method.

Once sigma is known, Gaussian deconvolution is exact at the level of
characteristic functions. The recovered law is uniquely determined; there
is no requirement that an empirical deconvolution be numerically stable.
Its minimum support point is b and its support width is eta n^2, so the
written endpoint recovery is valid because n is known and eta>0. After
rescaling, the mean lies strictly between zero and n. Therefore

 s=-log(1-E[I]/n)

is well-defined and unique. The recovered common s is the same in any two
putatively equal observation laws. The strict fourth-moment difference at
that s then rules out A/B aliasing. This order of argument avoids silently
assuming equal nuisance parameters under the two hypotheses.

5. LEMMA 4 AND THEOREM 5: POISSON PARAMETERS

The probability-generating-function identity is valid, including z=0 under
the usual convention 0^0=1. Equality on 0<z<1 alone suffices. Both bounded
mixing laws have entire Laplace transforms even if their bounds were not
known in advance. The identity theorem and characteristic-function
uniqueness therefore recover the mixing law. Nonnegativity ensures each
conditional Poisson distribution is defined. The theorem includes b=0
without a boundary problem.

A second check uses factorial moments: the count law determines
E[(N)_k]=E[Lambda^k] for every k. These are finite for bounded Lambda. If
two bounded mixing laws have these same moments, choose one compact
interval containing both supports. Polynomial approximation of continuous
functions on that interval then implies equality of the measures. This
gives the same injectivity conclusion independently of the displayed
analytic-continuation proof.

The exact mixing-law support endpoints recover b and eta just as in the
Gaussian case. The rescaled mean then recovers s and the fourth moment
recovers the structural label. The proof correctly uses the complete count
law, not merely four count moments or the fact that counts are unbounded.

6. TOTAL-VARIATION LIMITS

The convention used is TV(P,Q)=sup_A |P(A)-Q(A)|, ranging from zero to one.
For equal-variance normals the exact formula is 2 Phi(d/(2 sigma))-1,
where d is their mean separation. Integrating the normal density over an
interval of width d/sigma bounds this by d/(sigma sqrt(2 pi)), with the
constant in the file correct. Mixing over any coupling of the two bounded
intensities then yields the stated bound. The elementary product inequality
TV(P^m,Q^m)<=m TV(P,Q) is sufficient. The optimal equal-prior success is
(1+TV(P^m,Q^m))/2, so it tends to one half as sigma tends to infinity.

For Poisson laws, the independent-increment coupling gives probability of
a nonzero increment 1-exp(-|lambda-mu|), and TV is at most this coupling
mismatch probability. The bound by |lambda-mu| is correct. With common
background and gain the conditional-rate difference is at most eta n^2.
The same mixture and product arguments show that success tends to one half
as eta decreases to zero for any fixed finite m.

Both examples prove the claimed lack of a finite sample size that works
uniformly over the whole allowed parameter space for any fixed desired
advantage above random guessing. They do not contradict pointwise
identifiability at each finite sigma and each strictly positive eta. They
also do not claim these loose bounds are the best available bounds at a
specified detector setting.

7. UNKNOWN UNRESTRICTED ERROR COUNTEREXAMPLE

Use independent copies of I_A and I_B in the construction. Taking the
error under A to have law I_B-mu and the error under B to have law I_A-mu
is allowed by the unrestricted-error model. Convolution commutes, so the
observed laws agree exactly. Both errors have mean zero, compact support,
and the same variance. The noise laws themselves are different because
the signal laws are different and a common translation is injective.

This is an existence counterexample: it demonstrates that zero mean and a
known common variance alone are insufficient in general. It need not claim
an alias for every arbitrary preassigned noise variance. It does not use
dependent noise or vary an error within one fixed model. The explanation
of why this differs from common-error preservation is correct.

8. HISTORICAL BOUNDARIES AND REMAINING LIMITS

Primary sources inspected in the associated literature work establish that
the underlying deconvolution principles are already known:

Sapatinas (1995), DOI 10.1007/BF00773394, section 2 and Note 1,
https://www.ism.ac.jp/editsec/aism/pdf/047_3_0447.pdf
proves identifiability criteria that include Poisson mixtures. Its scope
is broader than the bounded-mixing-law lemma proved here.

Matias (2002), DOI 10.1051/ps:2002015, pages 272-275,
https://cmatias.perso.math.cnrs.fr/Docs/psns5.pdf
studies Gaussian deconvolution with unknown noise variance and the
restriction excluding a Gaussian convolution component. A compactly
supported signal necessarily satisfies that restriction.

The present structural/nuisance conclusion uses those standard ideas
together with the project's own support and moment properties. Its truth
does not imply that the underlying measurement-noise principles are novel.
