DETECTOR IDENTIFIABILITY BEYOND FIXED CALIBRATION
20 October 2026

Scope. These are population-distribution theorems for the exact 63-site
Gaussian-phase model already documented in science_completion. They do not
give a stable numerical reconstruction from a finite sample, an experimental
calibration, or a new general deconvolution principle. Compact-support
analyticity and Poisson-mixture identifiability are established methods.

Notation. G is A or B, n=63, s is a finite positive phase variance, and

  I_G(s) = |sum_{j=1}^n z_{G,j} exp(i sqrt(s) Z_j)|^2,

where the Z_j are independent standard Gaussians and |z_{G,j}|=1. The
observation frequency is the common clean null q0=pi/3. The established
identities are

  0 <= I_G(s) <= n^2,
  E I_G(s) = n(1-exp(-s)),
  E I_A(s)^k = E I_B(s)^k,  k=1,2,3,
  D4(s) = E I_A(s)^4-E I_B(s)^4 > 0.

The explicit positive D4 factorization is in the preceding proof record.

1. ANY COMMON INDEPENDENT ADDITIVE ERROR PRESERVES DISTINCTNESS

Theorem 1. Let P and Q be probability measures with compact support on R,
and let R be any probability measure on R. If P*R=Q*R, then P=Q. There are
no density, moment, symmetry, or nonvanishing-characteristic-function
assumptions on R.

Proof. Write characteristic functions phi_P, phi_Q, phi_R. Continuity and
phi_R(0)=1 imply phi_R(t) is nonzero on some real interval about zero.
Equality of the convolutions gives phi_P(t)=phi_Q(t) on that interval.
Compact support makes phi_P(z)-phi_Q(z) an entire function of complex z:
on every bounded subset of C its power series is dominated by an
exponential using the common finite bound on the supports. The identity
theorem therefore makes this entire function identically zero. Uniqueness
of characteristic functions implies P=Q. QED.

Application. For every fixed finite s>0, I_A(s) and I_B(s) remain different
after addition of any common independent error law, including Cauchy errors
or other errors without a fourth moment. The prior finite-fourth-moment
assumption was sufficient for a simple moment proof but was unnecessary for
identifiability. This result supplies no uniform separation bound over all
error laws.

2. FULL SUPPORT OF THE INTENSITY

Lemma 2. For every finite s>0, the support of the law of I_G(s) is exactly
the closed interval [0,n^2].

Proof. A Gaussian reduced modulo 2pi has a strictly positive density on the
whole circle. Independence therefore gives full support on the n-torus
for the random phase vector; multiplication by the fixed z_{G,j} is a
torus translation and preserves that property. The continuous function

  (theta_1,...,theta_n) -> |sum_j exp(i theta_j)|^2

has a compact connected image, hence a closed interval. Its maximum n^2
is attained by aligned phases. Its minimum zero is attained by the n-th
roots of unity (n>=2). Its image is therefore [0,n^2]. Every neighborhood
of an attained value has a nonempty open preimage and consequently positive
probability under the full-support phase law. QED.

This lemma needs finite positive s. At s=0 the common-null intensity is
identically zero. The limiting fully uniform phase law also needs separate
treatment for A/B identification, because it erases the fixed phases.

3. UNKNOWN GAUSSIAN READOUT PARAMETERS CANNOT CREATE AN EXACT A/B ALIAS

Theorem 3. Consider the family of observation laws

  Y = b + eta I_G(s) + sigma Z,

where Z is a standard Gaussian independent of the intensity, b is any real
offset, eta>0, sigma>=0, 0<s<infinity, and n and the two candidate structures
are known. The complete law of Y uniquely identifies G, s, b, eta, and
sigma. In particular two parameter tuples, even with different nuisance
parameters under A and B, cannot give the same observation law.

Proof. Put V=b+eta I_G(s), whose support is [b,b+eta n^2] by Lemma 2. Its
moment generating function M_V(t) is finite for every real t. For t>0,

  b t <= log M_V(t) <= (b+eta n^2)t.

Since M_Y(t)=exp(sigma^2 t^2/2) M_V(t), the observed law determines

  sigma^2 = lim_{t -> +infinity} 2 log M_Y(t) / t^2.

It then determines phi_V(t)=phi_Y(t) exp(sigma^2 t^2/2). Characteristic
function uniqueness determines the law of V. Its support endpoints give

  b = inf supp(V),
  eta = [sup supp(V)-inf supp(V)] / n^2.

The intensity law follows by the inverse affine transformation. Its mean
uniquely identifies

  s = -log(1-E[I]/n),

because n(1-exp(-s)) is strictly increasing on (0,infinity). Finally,
at that same s the two candidate intensity laws have different fourth
moments. This identifies G and completes the proof. QED.

This is identification from the exact population law. It is not a claim
that high-order tails, deconvolution, or support endpoints can be accurately
estimated with a modest finite sample. It does not license applying the
previous known-parameter sample-count formulas to estimated parameters
without a further analysis.

4. UNKNOWN POISSON BACKGROUND AND GAIN ARE ALSO IDENTIFIABLE

Lemma 4 (compact Poisson-mixture injectivity). A Poisson mixture whose
mixing law is supported on a finite interval in [0,infinity) uniquely
determines that mixing law, even when the support bound is not known.

Proof. If Lambda is the mixing variable, the count probability generating
function for 0<=z<=1 is

  E[z^N] = E[exp(-(1-z)Lambda)].

Equality of count distributions gives equality of the Laplace transforms
of the two mixing laws on 0<=t<=1. Each transform extends to an entire
function because each support is bounded. Their difference therefore
vanishes identically. Evaluate on the imaginary axis and use uniqueness
of characteristic functions to recover equality of the mixing laws. QED.

Theorem 5. In the family

  N | I_G(s) ~ Poisson(b + eta I_G(s)),

where b>=0, eta>0, 0<s<infinity, and n and the candidate structures are
known, the complete count law uniquely identifies G, s, b, and eta.

Proof. Lemma 4 identifies the law of Lambda=b+eta I_G(s). Lemma 2 shows
that its exact support is [b,b+eta n^2]. Its two endpoints identify b and
eta. Transforming back gives the intensity law, whose mean identifies s
and whose fourth moment identifies G, as in Theorem 3. QED.

The theorem includes b=0. It does not require that gain and background
have been calibrated externally for population identifiability, but the
finite-sample guarantees derived elsewhere still assume known models and
known detector parameters.

5. WHY THESE IDENTIFIABILITY THEOREMS DO NOT GIVE UNIFORM FINITE-SAMPLE
   PERFORMANCE

Gaussian example. For common b, eta and sigma>0, let P_sigma and Q_sigma
be the two Gaussian-readout laws. Couple arbitrary intensity realizations
x,y in [0,n^2]. The total variation between equal-variance normal laws is

  2 Phi(|eta(x-y)|/(2sigma))-1
  <= |eta(x-y)|/(sigma sqrt(2pi)).

The inequality follows by bounding the standard-normal density by its
maximum 1/sqrt(2pi). Convexity of total variation under mixing gives

  TV(P_sigma,Q_sigma) <= min(1, eta n^2/(sigma sqrt(2pi))).

For m independent observations, the elementary product bound gives
TV(P_sigma^m,Q_sigma^m)<=m TV(P_sigma,Q_sigma). Therefore, for every fixed
finite m, the optimal equal-prior success probability tends to 1/2 as
sigma tends to infinity. There can be no uniform finite sample size for
all allowed Gaussian noise scales.

Poisson example. For lambda>=mu, couple Poisson(lambda) to Poisson(mu)
by adding an independent Poisson(lambda-mu) variable. This gives

  TV(Poisson(lambda),Poisson(mu))
  <= 1-exp(-|lambda-mu|) <= |lambda-mu|.

Mixing and |I_A-I_B|<=n^2 give TV(N_A,N_B)<=min(1,eta n^2). Hence at any
fixed finite m the optimal success tends to 1/2 as eta tends to zero.
Strict identifiability at every positive gain does not prevent this limit.

6. AN UNRESTRICTED UNKNOWN ADDITIVE ERROR LAW IS A REAL OBSTRUCTION

Theorem 1 assumes the SAME error law is used in the two comparisons. If an
arbitrary unknown error law can vary as a nuisance parameter between the
candidate explanations, A/B identification from a single output law can
fail, even with independent, zero-mean errors of finite support.

Proof by construction. Fix s>0 and let mu=E I_A(s)=E I_B(s). In model A,
take an independent error E_A with law I_B(s)-mu. In model B, take an
independent error E_B with law I_A(s)-mu. Both observed laws are then
the law of I_A(s)+I_B(s)-mu with independent summands. The error means
are zero, and their variances also agree because the first two intensity
moments agree. Thus knowing only zero mean and variance of the errors
does not remove this exact ambiguity. QED.

This obstruction does not contradict Theorem 1: E_A and E_B are different
laws. It also does not contradict Theorems 3 and 5, whose specified
Gaussian or Poisson detector families supply the extra structure needed
for joint identifiability.
